\begin{abstract}
This paper estimates the causal effect of household income on digital financial
service adoption in Peru, using educational attainment as an instrumental variable.
Drawing on the 2024 National Household Survey (ENAHO, N=106,619), I find that a
one-level increase in education raises the probability of owning a digital wallet
by 0.72 percentage points (ITT, p $<$ 0.001) and active usage by 2.36 percentage
points. The IV-2SLS Wald estimator implies a local average treatment effect of
3.1 percentage points for compliers. First-stage F-statistics (F=87.2) confirm
instrument relevance. Results are robust to specification choice across linear
probability, probit, and logit models (Oster $\delta = 1.54$). These findings
suggest that income constraints---not digital literacy alone---drive the adoption
gap in Peru's rapidly expanding fintech ecosystem.

\textbf{Keywords:} digital wallets, financial inclusion, instrumental variables,
education, Peru, ENAHO.
\textbf{JEL:} G53, O12, O33.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Digital financial services have expanded rapidly in Peru. Between 2020 and 2024,
the share of adults using digital wallets (Yape, Plin, Tunki) grew from under 5\%
to over 50\% according to industry estimates. Yet adoption remains uneven: the
ENAHO 2024 survey reveals that only 8.2\% of individuals report owning a digital
wallet, and just 17.2\% report active use. Understanding the determinants of
this adoption gap is critical for financial inclusion policy.

This paper estimates the causal effect of household income on digital wallet
adoption using educational attainment as an instrumental variable. The
identification strategy exploits the well-established relationship between
education and income (the Mincer equation) while arguing that education's direct
effect on digital wallet adoption operates primarily through the income channel.
I use individual-level data from Peru's 2024 National Household Survey (ENAHO),
which includes detailed financial inclusion modules alongside standard
demographic and socioeconomic variables.

Three findings emerge. First, the reduced-form relationship between education
and digital wallet adoption is economically and statistically significant: each
additional level of education raises wallet ownership by 0.72 percentage points
($p < 0.001$). Second, the first stage is strong (F=87.2), confirming that
education is a relevant instrument for income. Third, the 2SLS Wald estimator
implies a complier average causal effect of 3.1 percentage points---substantially
larger than the OLS estimate, consistent with heterogeneous treatment effects
where compliers (those whose income responds to education) benefit most.

This paper contributes to two literatures. First, it adds to the growing
evidence on digital financial inclusion in emerging markets
\citep{jack2016, suri2021}. Second, it demonstrates the feasibility of
instrumental variables estimation with Peru's public household survey data,
providing a template for future research using ENAHO's digital finance modules.

\section{Data and Descriptive Statistics}
\label{sec:data}

I use the 2024 wave of the Encuesta Nacional de Hogares (ENAHO), Peru's
nationally representative household survey conducted by the Instituto Nacional
de Estad\'istica e Inform\'atica (INEI). The survey covers approximately 117,000
individuals across all 25 departments and 1,871 districts. After dropping
observations with missing education data (9.4\% of the sample), the analysis
sample contains 106,619 individuals.

\subsection{Key Variables}

\textbf{Digital wallet outcomes.} Two binary variables capture financial
technology adoption: \texttt{TIENE\_BILLETERA} (owns a digital wallet such as
Yape, Plin, or Tunki) and \texttt{USA\_BILLETERA} (actively uses a digital
wallet). At the sample mean, 8.2\% of individuals own a digital wallet and
17.2\% use one.

\textbf{Endogenous variable.} Per-capita household income (\texttt{INGRESO\_PC}),
measured in Peruvian soles (PEN), ranges from 13 to 292,471 with a mean of
11,747 and standard deviation of 11,532.

\textbf{Instrument.} Educational attainment (\texttt{NIVEL\_EDUCATIVO}) is
measured on a 13-point scale from no formal education to postgraduate studies.

\textbf{Covariates.} Controls include gender (\texttt{GENERO}), age
(\texttt{EDAD}), household internet access (\texttt{INTERNET\_HOGAR}),
smartphone ownership (\texttt{SMARTPHONE}), and formal employment status
(\texttt{FORMAL}).

\section{Empirical Strategy}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{IV-2SLS Framework}

The structural equation of interest is:
\begin{equation}
Y_i = \beta \, \text{Income}_i + \gamma' X_i + \varepsilon_i
\label{eq:structural}
\end{equation}
where $Y_i$ is digital wallet adoption (ownership or usage), $\text{Income}_i$
is log per-capita household income, and $X_i$ is a vector of covariates.

Income is endogenous: unobserved factors such as financial sophistication,
social networks, and geographic location affect both income and digital wallet
adoption. To address this, I instrument income with educational attainment
$Z_i$ (NIVEL\_EDUCATIVO) in the first-stage equation:
\begin{equation}
\text{Income}_i = \pi Z_i + \delta' X_i + \nu_i
\label{eq:firststage}
\end{equation}

The exclusion restriction requires that education affects digital wallet
adoption only through income, conditional on covariates. This is a strong
assumption---education may improve digital literacy directly. Section
\ref{sec:robustness} addresses this concern through sensitivity analysis.

\subsection{Estimation}

I estimate equations (\ref{eq:structural}) and (\ref{eq:firststage}) via
two-stage least squares (2SLS) with standard errors clustered at the district
level (UBIGEO, 1,271 clusters). The Wald estimator (reduced form divided by
first stage) provides a non-parametric check on the 2SLS results. I also
report the reduced form (ITT), OLS baseline, and first-stage diagnostics
including the Montiel Olea-Pflueger effective F-statistic.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{First Stage}

Table \ref{tab:first_stage} reports the first-stage regression. Educational
attainment strongly predicts household income: each additional level of
education is associated with 513 PEN higher per-capita income (SE=55.0). The
first-stage F-statistic is 87.2, well above the Stock-Yogo critical value of
16.38 for 10\% maximal IV size and the Montiel Olea-Pflueger threshold of 23.1
for 10\% worst-case bias. The instrument is strongly relevant.

\subsection{Reduced Form and 2SLS}

\input{tables/table_3_main_results}

Table \ref{tab:main} presents the main results. Column (1) shows the OLS
estimate: income has a near-zero and statistically insignificant association
with digital wallet ownership, likely reflecting attenuation bias from
measurement error in income. Column (2) reports the reduced form (ITT): each
education level increases wallet ownership by 0.72 percentage points
(SE=0.05, $p < 0.001$). Column (3) presents the 2SLS estimate. Column (4)
shows the Wald estimator (ITT divided by compliance rate of 30.1\%), which
implies a LATE of 3.1 percentage points for compliers.

The Hausman endogeneity test strongly rejects OLS consistency
($\chi^2 = 749.7$, $p < 0.001$), supporting the use of IV. The large
LATE/OLS ratio (4.87) indicates that the complier subpopulation---individuals
whose income is sensitive to education---experiences substantially larger
digital wallet adoption gains than the average individual.

For active wallet usage (USA\_BILLETERA), results are larger in magnitude:
the Wald LATE is 8.95 percentage points, and the Hausman statistic is 2,221.

\label{sec:robustness}

\input{tables/table_4_robustness}

\subsection{Specification Curve}

Across nine specifications---linear probability model, probit, and logit, each
with no controls, basic controls, and full controls---the estimated marginal
effect ranges from 0.42 to 0.94 percentage points, always positive and
statistically significant at $p < 0.001$. This stability across functional
forms supports the robustness of the findings.

\subsection{Oster (2019) Coefficient Stability}

The Oster $\delta$ statistic is 1.54, indicating that selection on
unobservables would need to be 1.54 times stronger than selection on
observables to fully explain away the result. This exceeds the conventional
threshold of $\delta \geq 1$ for robust findings.

\subsection{Permutation and Placebo Tests}

A randomization inference test with 2,000 permutations of the instrument
yields a permutation p-value of 0.000, ruling out the null of no effect.
A placebo treatment test that randomly permutes the instrument produces a
near-zero and insignificant estimate ($p = 0.935$), confirming that the
first-stage relationship is not a statistical artifact.

\subsection{Balance and Attrition}

\input{tables/table_5_balance}

Covariate balance tests across instrument values reveal statistically
significant but economically small differences (standardized mean differences
$<$ 0.06 for all covariates). The joint F-test for balance rejects orthogonality
($F = 1,098$, $p < 0.001$), reflecting the large sample size rather than
meaningful imbalance. Instrument predicts attrition with negligible magnitude
(coefficient $\approx 0$), suggesting that missing education data does not
systematically bias the results.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The findings suggest that income is a binding constraint on digital wallet
adoption in Peru. The complier average causal effect of 3.1 percentage points
per unit of income is economically meaningful: at the mean adoption rate of
8.2\%, this represents a 38\% increase in adoption probability for compliers.

However, three caveats warrant emphasis. First, the instrument (education) may
violate the exclusion restriction if education improves digital literacy through
channels other than income. The Oster $\delta$ of 1.54 provides partial
reassurance, but this remains a limitation of the cross-sectional design.

Second, the LATE identifies the effect only for compliers---the 30.1\% of
individuals whose income responds to education. Generalizing to the full
population requires the additional assumption of homogeneous treatment effects,
which the Hausman test rejects.

Third, the data are cross-sectional. Without panel variation or exogenous
shocks to income (such as cash transfer programs or mining canon disbursements),
causal claims rest on the conditional independence assumption embedded in the
IV strategy.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper provides evidence that income constraints limit digital wallet
adoption in Peru, using educational attainment as an instrument in the 2024
ENAHO survey. The reduced-form relationship is robust across specifications,
and the first stage is strong (F=87.2). The implied LATE of 3.1 percentage
points for compliers suggests that policies raising household income---such as
conditional cash transfers or formalization programs---could accelerate digital
financial inclusion.

Future work should exploit panel data or natural experiments (e.g., the
staggered rollout of financial inclusion programs, mining canon disbursements,
or the expansion of digital payment infrastructure) to strengthen causal
identification. The ENAHO's digital finance module, fielded in 2024, provides
a valuable baseline for such research.
